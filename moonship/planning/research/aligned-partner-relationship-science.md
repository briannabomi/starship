# Aligned-partner quiz: relationship-science research

## Purpose and scope

This document translates relationship and sexuality research into guardrails and design recommendations for a low-resistance lead-magnet quiz. The quiz can help a person name preferences, needs, boundaries, and likely areas for conversation. It cannot identify a uniquely correct partner, diagnose attachment or sexual dysfunction, predict whether a relationship will succeed, or replace individualized medical or mental-health care.

The intended output is therefore a **reflection profile**, not a clinical assessment or compatibility verdict. Any score should describe the respondent's current self-report in plain language and invite conversation. It should not label a person or partner as healthy/unhealthy, secure/insecure, compatible/incompatible, or destined to stop having sex.

## Executive findings

1. **Relationship processes are more useful than a generic “same personality” match.** Across 43 longitudinal couple datasets, the strongest relationship-specific self-report predictors of relationship quality included perceived partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived partner satisfaction, and conflict. Personality similarity, by contrast, has generally explained little incremental variance in relationship satisfaction. The quiz should emphasize how people want to relate, negotiate, and repair—not promise a personality soulmate.
2. **Sexual communication matters, especially its quality.** A meta-analysis of 93 studies (38,499 people) found sexual communication positively associated with relationship satisfaction (r = .37) and sexual satisfaction (r = .43). Communication quality had stronger associations than frequency alone. These are associations, not proof that talking more automatically causes satisfaction.
3. **Desire discrepancy is common and relational, not evidence that one partner is broken.** Professional guidance treats discrepancy as a couple-level difference that can involve desired frequency, context, motivation, and the meaning attached to sex. The useful target is how partners understand and navigate the difference while protecting consent and autonomy.
4. **Responsiveness has three practical elements: understanding, validation, and care.** Sexual responsiveness additionally requires mutuality and respect for boundaries. “Meeting needs” must never be framed as entitlement to another person's body.
5. **Attachment anxiety and avoidance correlate with poorer relationship outcomes, but attachment is dimensional and context-sensitive.** A short marketing quiz should not assign attachment diagnoses or imply permanence. It can ask about preferences under stress—reassurance, space, directness, repair—and report them neutrally.
6. **No consensual relationship structure should be treated as inherently superior.** A 2025 meta-analysis of 35 studies (N = 24,489) found no overall difference in relationship or sexual satisfaction between monogamous and consensually non-monogamous participants. Fit depends on informed, explicit agreements, communication, and consent—not structure alone.
7. **Safety is a prerequisite, not a compatibility dimension.** Coercion, violence, disregard for consent, or punished boundaries should never be softened into “different styles.” Results should point to support resources when safety concerns are disclosed.

## Evidence by product domain

### 1. Compatibility and “aligned partners”

Compatibility is best presented as alignment across **specific, negotiable or non-negotiable domains**, rather than a single latent type.

High-value domains for reflection:

- relationship intention and commitment expectations;
- relationship structure and exclusivity agreements;
- affection, intimacy, and preferred ways of giving/receiving care;
- sexual interest, meaning, communication, initiation, and flexibility;
- autonomy, togetherness, and time/space needs;
- conflict engagement, regulation, and repair;
- life direction: children, home, money, work, faith/spirituality, health, and community;
- boundaries, consent, privacy, and digital expectations;
- willingness to discuss and renegotiate agreements.

Why this framing: Joel et al.'s multi-lab project found relationship-specific perceptions more predictive than individual-difference variables, although even their longitudinal prediction was limited (up to 18% of later relationship-quality variance). This is strong reason not to turn a quiz score into a forecast. In large couple studies, personality similarity explains little variance after actor and partner characteristics are considered; a 2023 dyadic study likewise found trait/facet similarity was not robustly associated with satisfaction.

An ideal-partner match can still be a reflection prompt. A three-year newlywed study found that perceived partner meeting one's ideal standards was associated with acceptance and satisfaction, with nonlinear patterns. It does **not** establish a universal ideal or show that matching quiz archetypes causes success.

**Implementation implication:** calculate independent domain profiles and priority flags rather than one “compatibility percentage.” Distinguish:

- `must have / cannot accept` (boundary or deal-breaker),
- `strong preference`,
- `flexible / negotiable`, and
- `unsure / want to explore`.

A mismatch on a flexible preference is categorically different from a conflict over consent, children, or relationship structure.

### 2. Sexual desire and desire discrepancy

Sexual desire discrepancy is not synonymous with low desire and is not fully captured by intercourse frequency. The European Society for Sexual Medicine position statement emphasizes variability in definition and measurement and says more research is needed on mechanisms and clinical guidance. Older large-sample findings link discrepancies between desired and actual frequency with relationship outcomes, but results vary by gender and relationship duration and do not justify deterministic claims.

Ask about multiple components without assuming partnered intercourse:

- how important sexual connection feels in a relationship;
- preferred frequency/rhythm, with “varies” and “not currently important” options;
- spontaneous versus context-dependent interest;
- preferred initiation and response to initiation;
- meaning of sexual connection (pleasure, play, closeness, reassurance, exploration, etc.);
- comfort discussing interests, limits, pain, health, or changing desire;
- preferred response to a “not now”;
- openness to collaboratively exploring mutually wanted forms of intimacy.

Avoid claims such as “your mismatch causes sexlessness.” Desire can shift with stress, relationship context, health, medication, pregnancy/postpartum, menopause, disability, trauma, identity, and many other factors. The quiz should normalize variation and recommend qualified care where distress, pain, or sudden changes are present.

**Implementation implication:** report a “sexual connection conversation profile,” not libido rank. Suggested neutral output: “You may value predictable, direct conversations about sexual connection and a partner who can hear both interest and limits without pressure.”

### 3. Sexual and relationship communication

Mallory's 2022 meta-analysis found moderate positive associations between sexual communication and satisfaction. Quality—openness, positive affect, satisfaction with the discussion, and emotional safety—was more strongly associated with relationship and sexual satisfaction than frequency alone. The authors explicitly note directionality cannot be established.

Useful quiz constructs:

- directness versus gradual/contextual discussion;
- emotional safety when naming wants and limits;
- curiosity rather than mind-reading;
- ability to receive a “no” without punishment;
- ability to revisit a conversation after conflict;
- comfort acknowledging changes over time;
- collaborative problem-solving without making one person the problem.

**Implementation implication:** prefer situational items over identity labels. For example: “When something intimate isn't working for me, I most want a partner who…” followed by behaviorally distinct options. Do not score mere frequency of talking as inherently better.

### 4. Responsiveness, needs, and mutuality

Relationship science commonly defines perceived partner responsiveness through:

- **understanding**: the partner seems to grasp one's experience and core self;
- **validation**: the partner treats that experience as meaningful and legitimate;
- **care**: the partner communicates warmth and concern.

Qualitative research on sexual need responsiveness adds mutuality and boundary respect. Responsiveness is not compliance, self-abandonment, or sexual obligation. Autonomy-support research also associates feeling free from pressure and supported in one's choices with need fulfillment and satisfaction.

Useful dimensions:

- “I want my partner to understand before offering solutions”;
- “I want needs to be explicit rather than inferred”;
- “I need room to decline without managing my partner's emotions”;
- “I value creative options that protect both people's limits”;
- “I want regular check-ins because needs change.”

**Implementation implication:** results should describe both what the respondent hopes to receive and what healthy reciprocity asks them to offer. Avoid a consumer-style list of what an “aligned partner must provide.”

### 5. Attachment-related needs

Adult romantic attachment is often studied along continuous anxiety and avoidance dimensions. A meta-analysis found both dimensions negatively associated with relationship satisfaction and commitment, with stronger negative associations in longer relationships in cross-sectional samples. Large-scale longitudinal prediction also identifies attachment anxiety and avoidance among relevant individual predictors, but relationship-specific variables tend to be more predictive.

For a nonclinical quiz:

- do not call the result an attachment “diagnosis”;
- do not infer childhood cause;
- do not imply a fixed type or a doomed anxious/avoidant pairing;
- do ask about observable needs under stress: reassurance, proximity, time to regulate, clarity, consistency, independence, and repair;
- allow apparently mixed needs (e.g., wanting reassurance and space).

Suggested result wording: “When connection feels uncertain, you may prefer clear reassurance and a specific plan to reconnect.” This is more actionable and less stigmatizing than “You are anxiously attached.”

### 6. Boundaries, consent, and safety

WHO defines sexual health positively and relationally: it includes physical, emotional, mental, and social well-being, a respectful approach, pleasurable and safe experiences, and freedom from coercion, discrimination, and violence. This should anchor all sexual-content UX.

Distinguish:

- a **preference** (“I enjoy daily texting”),
- a **request** (“Would you text when plans change?”),
- an **agreement** (mutually chosen expectation), and
- a **boundary** (what a person will or will not participate in, and what they will do to care for themselves).

Consent must be freely given, specific to the activity/context, and revocable. A person can change their mind. Quiz copy must not imply that love, partnership, prior consent, or responsiveness creates sexual access.

Red-flag disclosures—fear of saying no, retaliation, coercion, surveillance, isolation, violence—should not be scored as compatibility. Provide a discreet safety note and appropriate crisis/support links based on launch geography. Do not instruct someone to confront an abusive partner or jointly negotiate when doing so may increase risk.

### 7. Relationship structures and inclusion

Offer options without hierarchy: monogamy, monogamish/open agreements, polyamory or other consensual non-monogamy, celibate/asexual-spectrum arrangements, questioning/unsure, and a write-in where safe. Separate identity, behavior, and desired agreement. Never conflate consensual non-monogamy with cheating; consent and agreement are the distinction.

The 2025 meta-analysis found null overall differences in relationship and sexual satisfaction by monogamous versus non-monogamous orientation. Therefore, the quiz may identify **agreement alignment** but should not award a health score to a structure.

Use gender-inclusive, orientation-inclusive language:

- “partner” rather than husband/wife by default;
- do not presume a two-person dyad where the question does not require it;
- do not presume intercourse, cohabitation, marriage, parenthood, or sexual attraction;
- allow “not applicable,” “prefer not to answer,” and “still exploring.”

## Recommended quiz model

### Profile axes

Use 6–8 named, independent axes. A defensible initial set is:

1. **Commitment & life direction** — intention, consistency, future planning, major values.
2. **Closeness & autonomy** — connection, independence, space, daily rhythm.
3. **Communication & repair** — directness, regulation, conflict follow-through.
4. **Care & responsiveness** — understanding, validation, warmth, reciprocity.
5. **Sexual connection** — importance, desire context, initiation, communication, flexibility.
6. **Consent & boundaries** — comfort naming limits, receiving limits, bodily autonomy.
7. **Relationship agreements** — exclusivity/structure, privacy, transparency, renegotiation.
8. **Growth & exploration** — curiosity, change tolerance, willingness to seek support.

Safety items should be unscored and handled separately.

### Item-design principles

- Ask one idea per item; avoid “honest and affectionate” double-barreled options.
- Use behavior and scenarios, not moralized adjectives.
- Include ambivalence and context: “it depends,” “varies,” “not applicable,” and “unsure.”
- Ask importance separately from preference. A difference matters more when the domain is non-negotiable.
- Avoid forced ranking where two needs can coexist.
- Keep explicit sexual questions optional and disclose the topic before entry.
- Use progressive disclosure: begin with intention and relational preferences; place sensitive content later.
- Let respondents skip without blocking results.
- Do not infer a partner's traits from one person's answers.
- If partner comparison is later added, each partner should answer independently and consent to sharing.

### Scoring and result generation

- Score domain tendencies, not health or worth.
- Preserve uncertainty: low item coverage should produce “not enough information,” not a confident label.
- Treat a deal-breaker as a flag, not as a large numeric penalty hidden inside an average.
- Do not collapse opposing ends into good/bad. Direct and gradual communication can both work; the report should name likely needs and conversation prompts.
- Do not claim psychometric validation unless reliability, construct validity, and external validation have actually been studied in the intended population.
- If archetypes are used for memorability, make them editorial summaries layered over domain scores. State that people may resonate with more than one and can change over time.

## Claims and copy guardrails

| Avoid | Evidence-aligned alternative |
|---|---|
| “Discover your perfect partner.” | “Clarify the qualities, agreements, and relationship practices that may fit you best.” |
| “This is why your relationships stop having sex.” | “Differences in desire, context, communication, health, and relationship dynamics can all affect sexual connection.” |
| “You are incompatible.” | “This domain may need an explicit conversation, especially if it is non-negotiable for either person.” |
| “Your attachment style is…” | “When connection feels uncertain, you may tend to want…” |
| “You need a high-libido partner.” | “You may value a partner who can discuss sexual rhythm openly and navigate differences without pressure.” |
| “Your score predicts relationship success.” | “Your profile is a starting point for self-reflection and conversation, not a prediction.” |
| “Partners should meet each other's sexual needs.” | “Partners can listen and respond with care while respecting both people's autonomy and boundaries.” |
| “Monogamy/open relationships are healthier.” | “Health depends on consent, fit, communication, and honored agreements; no structure is inherently superior.” |

### Required result-page disclaimer

> This quiz is for education and self-reflection. It is not a diagnostic or validated compatibility assessment and cannot predict relationship outcomes. Your needs and preferences can change with context and over time. Consent, safety, and respected boundaries matter in every relationship. If sexual concerns cause distress, involve pain, or include pressure or fear, consider support from an appropriately qualified professional or local support service.

## Validation roadmap before stronger claims

1. Conduct cognitive interviews across diverse genders, orientations, cultures, abilities, ages, and relationship structures to check interpretation and emotional safety.
2. Have relationship/sex-therapy subject-matter experts review item relevance and omissions.
3. Pilot with adequate sample size; inspect missingness, ceiling/floor effects, item redundancy, and completion drop-off around sensitive content.
4. Test internal structure and reliability by domain. Reliability alone does not establish validity.
5. Test convergent/discriminant validity using appropriate validated constructs, without presenting the lead magnet as equivalent to a clinical scale.
6. Test measurement invariance before comparing demographic groups.
7. If claiming partner-fit utility, validate prospectively with independent dyadic data. A one-person cross-sectional quiz cannot support such a claim.
8. Version scoring rules and copy; revalidate after material item or population changes.

## Bottom line for implementation

The evidence supports a quiz that helps users articulate priorities and initiate safer, higher-quality conversations. It does not support deterministic matchmaking, sexlessness causation claims, or clinical labels. The most credible promise is: **“Name the relationship practices, agreements, and forms of connection that matter most to you—and leave with language for discussing them.”**
