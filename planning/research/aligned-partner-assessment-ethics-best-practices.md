# Aligned-partner assessment: ethics and measurement best practices

## Purpose and product boundary

The proposed lead magnet should be positioned as a **non-diagnostic, low-stakes self-reflection quiz** that helps an adult articulate preferences, boundaries, and conversation topics. It must not claim to identify a uniquely “aligned” partner, predict relationship success, diagnose attachment/sexual dysfunction, or determine whether a current partner is right or wrong.

That boundary follows the central measurement principle in the *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*: score interpretations need evidence for their intended use, and fairness includes equitable treatment, absence of measurement bias, access to the construct, and validity of the proposed interpretation ([AERA/APA/NCME Standards](https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/9780935302356.pdf)). A marketing quiz based on a founder workbook will not initially have the validation evidence required for clinical, predictive, or high-stakes claims. Its valid early use is guided reflection.

Recommended pre-quiz statement:

> This reflection helps you name relationship preferences and possible conversation topics. It is not therapy, a diagnosis, or a prediction of compatibility. People and relationships change; use your result as a starting point, not a label.

Recommended result-page reminder:

> This result describes patterns in the answers you chose today. It does not define you or evaluate your partner. Keep what feels useful, revise what does not, and talk directly with partners rather than treating this result as proof.

## 1. Construct design

### Define constructs before writing or importing items

Create a construct map and tag every workbook question to exactly one primary construct. A practical initial model is:

1. **Connection and emotional presence** — desired attention, affection, responsiveness, and repair.
2. **Autonomy and togetherness** — preferred balance of independence, shared time, and decision-making.
3. **Communication and conflict** — preferences for directness, pacing, reassurance, reflection, and repair.
4. **Affection and sexual connection** — preferences around affection, erotic connection, initiation, frequency discussions, and novelty, without implying an ideal amount of sex.
5. **Commitment and relationship structure** — expectations about exclusivity/non-exclusivity, labels, future planning, and agreements.
6. **Values and daily-life partnership** — money, care work, family, spirituality, work, social life, and shared responsibilities.
7. **Boundaries, consent, and safety** — ability to express, hear, and revisit limits and agreements. This should be educational/referral-oriented, not blended into a “compatibility” score.

For each construct, document: definition; what is explicitly outside it; intended use; target adult population; item IDs; response scale; scoring rule; and result claims allowed. The Standards call for explicit documentation of purpose, intended population, fairness, reliability/precision, and validity evidence, with scrutiny proportional to the stakes ([AERA/APA/NCME](https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf)).

Avoid collapsing everything into one “alignment score.” Relationship preferences are multidimensional and sometimes context-dependent. A profile of dimensions is more honest and actionable than a false 87% compatibility number.

### Item-writing rules

Use one concrete idea per item. Pew’s survey-method guidance emphasizes clear, specific language; exhaustive, mutually exclusive response choices; avoiding double-barreled questions; pretesting; and awareness that wording and order change answers ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/)). Apply these rules:

- Ask about a defined context or preference: “When tension comes up, I usually prefer…” rather than “I communicate well.”
- Do not conflate desire and behavior: “How important is affectionate touch to you?” and “How often do you currently experience it?” are different questions.
- Do not conflate sex and love, sex and relationship health, or lower desire and dysfunction.
- Avoid absolutes such as “always,” “never,” “normal,” “healthy couples,” or “a real partner.”
- Avoid leading premises such as “When relationships inevitably lose passion…”
- Use balanced options with neutral valence. Do not make the commercially preferred answer sound mature, spiritual, healed, or courageous.
- Include “It depends,” “I’m not sure yet,” “Not applicable,” and “Prefer not to answer” when truthful.
- Keep ordinal scales ordered; randomize independent option lists where order has no semantic meaning. Pew notes primacy/recency and question-order effects ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/)).
- Put easy, non-sensitive questions first. Introduce intimate items with a content note and a visible skip option.
- Ask relationship status only if routing genuinely needs it; allow single, dating, partnered, multiple relationships, between relationships, and self-description.

### Reduce shame and social-desirability pressure

Sensitive questions invite answers that sound socially acceptable. Normalize the full range before the question: “People vary widely in how much sexual connection matters to them.” Use self-administered interactions, neutral copy, and symmetric answer labels. Do not show “correct” answers or moral feedback. Pew specifically identifies social-desirability effects in sensitive questions and recommends response options that make honest answers easier ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/)).

## 2. Scoring and result archetypes

### Recommended scoring model

- Score each construct independently, preferably as a transparent mean of answered items transformed to 0–100 for display.
- Require a documented minimum number of answered items per dimension. If unmet, show “Not enough answers yet,” not an imputed result.
- Treat “Prefer not to answer” and “Not applicable” as missing, never as midpoint or zero.
- Reverse-score only carefully reviewed items; excessive reverse wording harms comprehension.
- Do not weight questions based on marketing value. Any weight must have a construct rationale and later empirical evidence.
- Keep raw dimension values behind human-readable bands such as “more independent ↔ more intertwined.” Do not imply clinical cutoffs.
- If the quiz asks “what I want” and “what I currently experience,” score them separately and describe the difference as a **conversation gap**, never a deficit or incompatibility.
- Never calculate couple compatibility from one person’s report about another. If a future two-person mode exists, each person consents and answers independently; comparisons describe similarities/differences without ranking the relationship.

### Archetypes should be summaries, not types

Archetypes can improve recall, but should be generated from the strongest one or two preferences and presented as a “current reflection.” Requirements:

- Use descriptive, non-hierarchical names (for example, “Steady Co-Creator” or “Spacious Connector”), never pathologizing or status-loaded labels.
- Give each archetype strengths, needs, possible friction, reflection questions, and language for a partner conversation.
- Avoid “your ideal partner is X.” Prefer “You may appreciate partners who can openly negotiate X.”
- State that people can match multiple summaries and can change by context or life stage.
- Show dimension detail so the name does not hide the scoring logic.
- Provide “This doesn’t feel like me” feedback and allow result regeneration after answer changes.
- Never infer gender, orientation, monogamy, trauma, diagnosis, libido disorder, or abuse from an archetype.

### Validation roadmap and claim ladder

Before launch: expert content review, inclusive-language review, workbook-to-construct traceability, cognitive interviews across diverse relationship structures and identities, and usability/accessibility testing. Pilot with the intended population and inspect missingness, completion, item distributions, internal consistency by dimension, and whether items behave differently across key groups. Do not optimize only for lead conversion.

After adequate samples: examine dimensional structure, score precision/reliability, test–retest stability over an appropriate interval, and relationships with clearly defined external measures. Analyze fairness and measurement invariance where feasible. Revise and re-pilot when items or scoring change. Publish a plain-language methodology/version note.

Claim ladder:

- **Allowed initially:** “summarizes your answers,” “highlights preferences,” “offers reflection prompts.”
- **Only after evidence:** “reliably measures [defined construct] in [population].”
- **Do not claim for this product:** “predicts your ideal partner,” “reveals why your relationship stopped having sex,” “diagnoses mismatch,” or “scientifically proves compatibility.”

## 3. Inclusive relationship and sexuality language

AASECT opposes education that pathologizes or prescribes sexual orientation, identity, or consensual sexual expression and recognizes diverse sexual experience ([AASECT position](https://www.aasect.org/position-sexual-expression-including-orientation-and-identity)). Therefore:

- Use “partner” and user-defined terms, not husband/wife, masculine/feminine role assumptions, or fixed pursuer/provider roles.
- Separate gender identity, sexual orientation, relationship structure, and sexual behavior; collect none unless needed.
- Support monogamous, consensually non-monogamous, queer, asexual-spectrum, aromantic-spectrum, disabled, long-distance, celibate, and culturally varied experiences without treating one as default.
- Say “sexual connection” or name the relevant behavior; do not assume intercourse, penetration, orgasm, frequency, or cohabitation.
- Avoid “high/low value,” “damaged,” “avoidant” as an unearned diagnosis, “frigid,” “sexless failure,” “normal libido,” and gender-essentialist claims.
- Distinguish consensual preferences from coercion. A boundary is not a mismatch to be overcome.
- Use plain, respectful, nonstigmatizing language and test it with the communities represented. CDC guidance recommends active verbs, accessible formats, cultural context, avoiding stereotypes and blame, and community involvement ([CDC equity-centered communication](https://www.cdc.gov/pcd/issues/2023/23_0061.htm); [CDC preferred terms](https://www.cdc.gov/health-communication/php/toolkit/preferred-terms.html)).

## 4. Consent, trauma-informed UX, and sexual topics

AASECT describes consent as clear, understandable, voluntary, specific, and withdrawable; pressure and manipulation are unacceptable ([AASECT consent statement](https://www.aasect.org/aasect-position-statement-consent-and-sexual-violence)). Reflect this in both educational content and the product experience.

- Use an adult-audience gate if sexual questions are included; do not knowingly collect minors’ sexual-preference data without a separate legal/safeguarding design.
- Before sensitive questions, explain what will be asked, why, whether it is stored, and that any item may be skipped.
- Provide a one-tap “skip this section” and neutral exit. Do not lower a score for skipping.
- Never ask for descriptions of assault, coercion, trauma, or illegal activity merely to personalize a lead magnet.
- Do not tell someone to confront a partner or disclose quiz answers when doing so may be unsafe.
- Avoid surprise sexual language, explicit imagery, shame, urgency, and “breakthrough” pressure.
- Use safety, transparency, choice, collaboration, empowerment, and cultural awareness as review criteria. These reflect SAMHSA’s trauma-informed principles ([SAMHSA](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/trauma-violence/trauma-informed-approaches-programs)).

If answers suggest fear, coercion, or unwanted sexual contact, do **not** score them into an archetype or diagnose abuse. Show a discreet, jurisdiction-appropriate support panel such as: “Pressure or fear is not a compatibility issue. You deserve support and can choose what happens next.” Provide an immediate close/hide option and current specialist resources. WHO emphasizes privacy, confidentiality, trained response, safety, and referral networks when assessing partner violence; a generic marketing quiz is not a substitute ([WHO guidelines](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241548595); [WHO implementation guidance](https://www.who.int/news/item/06-10-2022-preventing-intimate-partner-violence-improves-mental-health)).

## 5. Privacy and meaningful consent

Sex life and sexual orientation are especially sensitive data (and are “special category data” under GDPR where applicable) ([UK ICO GDPR guide](https://ico.org.uk/media/for-organisations/guide-to-data-protection/guide-to-the-general-data-protection-regulation-gdpr-1-1.pdf)). Even if a specific law does not apply, use the stricter product standard:

- Default to local/session-only scoring where possible. Collect the minimum needed to return a result.
- Separate quiz completion from email capture. Let users see the result without surrendering sensitive answers; if email delivery is offered, explain exactly what is sent.
- Use separate, unchecked consent for marketing. Never bundle “show my result” with newsletter consent.
- State purpose, data fields, storage location, retention period, processors/sharing, deletion method, and contact point in short just-in-time copy linked to a full notice.
- Do not put raw answers, archetypes, sexual data, or safety responses in ad pixels, analytics event names, URLs, referrers, logs, session replay, CRM profile fields, or email subject lines.
- Use pseudonymous aggregate analytics, suppress small cohorts, restrict staff access, encrypt in transit/at rest, define deletion, and test export/deletion flows.
- Do not sell or use intimate answers for ad targeting, model training, or unrelated personalization without genuinely optional, specific consent.
- Conduct a privacy-risk review across the full lifecycle from collection through disposal, consistent with NIST’s framing of privacy risk as problems individuals may experience from data processing ([NIST Privacy Framework](https://www.nist.gov/privacy-framework/getting-started-0)).

## 6. Avoiding manipulation in a lead magnet

The result must not manufacture insecurity to sell the cure. The FTC identifies interface tactics that obscure information, steer privacy choices, disguise promotion as independent content, or trick people into sharing data as dark patterns ([FTC report](https://www.ftc.gov/reports/bringing-dark-patterns-light); [FTC summary](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2022/09/ftc-report-shows-rise-sophisticated-dark-patterns-designed-trick-trap-consumers)).

Prohibit:

- fake countdowns, false scarcity, progress loss threats, confirm-shaming, prechecked marketing boxes, hidden skip/close controls, or result withholding after completion;
- alarming claims (“Your relationship is at risk”) unsupported by validated evidence;
- disguised advertorial recommendations or claims that the quiz independently chose a paid offer when the funnel always does;
- using a user’s sensitive answer verbatim in sales copy to pressure them;
- implying a purchase, consultation, or email signup is necessary to understand or fix their result.

Required CTA treatment: label paid and free next steps; make “No thanks” equally legible; distinguish general education from professional care; disclose commercial relationships; and offer useful no-purchase reflection prompts. Optimize for informed completion, comprehension, skips respected, deletion success, and reported usefulness—not conversion alone.

## 7. Result-content template

Each result should contain:

1. **Scope:** “Based on your answers today…” plus the non-diagnostic reminder.
2. **Preference profile:** dimension bars or plain-language ranges, including insufficient-answer states.
3. **What may support you:** behaviors a partner could willingly negotiate, not identity prescriptions.
4. **Possible friction:** neutral differences, with multiple explanations and no blame.
5. **Conversation starters:** “How do each of us like to reconnect after conflict?”; “What does satisfying affection or sexual connection mean to each of us right now?”
6. **Boundaries reminder:** agreement is mutual, specific, ongoing, and revisable; no result overrides consent.
7. **Agency:** edit answers, retake, delete, decline email, and say the result did not fit.
8. **Support routing:** general education, qualified relationship/sexual-health professionals, and discreet safety resources as separate paths.

## 8. Release acceptance checklist

- [ ] Every item maps to a defined construct and intended result statement.
- [ ] No item is double-barreled, leading, gender-essentialist, diagnostic, or based on a universal “normal.”
- [ ] Sensitive sections have advance notice, skip, neutral exit, and no scoring penalty.
- [ ] Results expose dimension logic and never claim a unique ideal partner or predicted success.
- [ ] Missing/NA/prefer-not-to-answer values are not silently scored.
- [ ] Result and CTA copy passed manipulation/dark-pattern review.
- [ ] Result access is not conditional on email or marketing consent.
- [ ] Sensitive values are absent from URLs, pixels, session replay, analytics payloads, logs, and CRM unless explicitly justified and protected.
- [ ] Privacy notice, retention, deletion, export, access controls, and incident handling are tested.
- [ ] Inclusive and trauma-informed reviews include people from intended audiences.
- [ ] Cognitive interviews and pilot findings are documented; changes increment the assessment version.
- [ ] Support links are current, discreet, localized where feasible, and reviewed by a qualified safety professional.
- [ ] Accessibility testing includes keyboard, screen reader, zoom/reflow, focus visibility, error recovery, and plain-language comprehension.

## Bottom-line implementation recommendation

Build a transparent multidimensional preference profile, not a partner-matching oracle. Give users control over sensitive questions and data, describe results tentatively, represent consensual relationship diversity without hierarchy, and keep safety signals outside marketing scores. The best lead magnet here earns trust by helping someone name their own questions—not by claiming authority over their identity or relationship.
